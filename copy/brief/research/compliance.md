## Findings (17)

### An ASA ruling already exists against a dog bed for exactly the emotional-manipulation angle this brief asks for

A&T International Ltd t/a Bella&Toby advertised: 'DON'T LET YOUR PET SUFFER WHILE YOU'RE AT WORK … 1 in 4 pets suffer from anxiety when home alone, but with Bella&Toby's Anti-stress bed, your pet won't be one of them!' The ASA held consumers would understand this as a claim that the bed reduced or eliminated separation anxiety. The company's evidence (a third-party mattress manufacturer site, a general dog-interest site on sleeping positions, and one customer testimonial) was rejected: the testimonial was 'subjective' and lacked the 'robust evidence required to support objective, scientific claims.' Breached CAP rules 3.1 (misleading), 3.7 (substantiation) and 12.1. Action: 'The ads must not appear again in their current form. We told A&T International Ltd t/a Bella&Toby not to claim that their beds reduced stress or anxiety unless they held adequate substantiation.'

Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/a-t-international-ltd-a22-1169887-at-international-ltd.html

**Action:** Do not write anything claiming a PawLunova bed reduces, eases, calms or eliminates anxiety, stress, separation anxiety or nervousness in a dog. This is the single closest precedent to this store and it was upheld. Also note the 'don't let your pet suffer' guilt-hook was part of what got ruled against — that exact emotional lever is on the record as non-compliant when tied to an unsubstantiated outcome.

### The ASA has ruled specifically on the words 'orthopaedic', 'pressure relief' and 'pain-free' applied to a bed

Origin Sleep UK Ltd advertised 'Absolute Pressure Relief […] Orthopaedic Back Support', 'Designed by orthopedics and engineered for pain-free sleep', 'Perfect for back & joint pain relief', 'Three Times More Support […] Enhanced Pressure Relief', '3x Greater Pressure Relief […] 2x Enhanced Back Support' and 'Ultimate Dynamic Back Support' claiming to 'alleviate back aches'. The ASA said it expected 'robust documentary evidence' consisting of 'clinical trials conducted on people'. Origin submitted no evidence and did not respond. All claims were held unsubstantiated and misleading. Breached CAP rules 3.1, 3.7, 3.33 (comparisons with identifiable competitors) and 12.1. Action: Origin must ensure they 'did not make claims about the orthopaedic and health properties' of their products unless 'fully substantiated'; referred to CAP's compliance team.

Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/origin-sleep-uk-ltd-a24-1253221-origin-sleep-uk-ltd.html

**Action:** Treat 'orthopaedic', 'pressure relief', 'pain-free' and any numeric support comparison ('3x more support') as objective health claims that require held clinical evidence BEFORE publication. The comparative claims also breached 3.33, so avoid '3x more supportive than ordinary beds' style lines entirely unless you hold the test data.

### There is a narrow, usable safe harbour: 'positional, temporary relief or comfort'

CAP's guidance on adjustable beds and chairs — the closest analogous product category — states that 'a well-supported posture might provide temporary pain relief to some conditions' and that products may claim to offer 'positional, temporary relief or comfort' where evidence supports it, but 'claims must not imply the product treats a condition or suggest relief exceeds temporary duration'. Claims that ARE off-limits without clinical substantiation include treating arthritis or rheumatism, 'improved circulation'/'improved blood supply', 'may improve the rate at which the body recovers from injury', and reducing joint inflammation. Rulings cited: Niagara Therapy (UK) Ltd 2005, Willowbrook Ltd 2009 (recommending chairs for arthritis pain ruled misleading), Dreamwell Ltd 2012.

Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/adjustable-beds-and-chairs.html

**Action:** This is the lane the copy must stay in. Write about comfort, support, posture, weight distribution, getting up more easily, undisturbed sleep — framed as what the bed DOES structurally, not what it CURES. Never bridge from the dog's arthritis to the bed's effect on the arthritis.

### Pain-relief claims for a non-medical product are treated as medicinal claims and are effectively unavailable

CAP: 'Claims to treat or alleviate pain are likely to be considered medicinal and marketers would need to ensure that any necessary licences and marking authorisations are held.' And: 'Marketers of devices that are not appropriately certified as a medical device should not make claims to treat, relieve or reduce pain (directly or indirectly).' On arthritis specifically: 'references to certain types of pain (arthritic pain) or some descriptions of pain (like chronic pain) could be understood as a reference to a condition for which medical supervision should be sought' — engaging rule 12.2, which prohibits discouraging essential treatment for conditions for which medical supervision should be sought.

Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/health-pain.html

**Action:** A dog bed is not a licensed veterinary medicine or a certified medical device, so pain claims are closed off — including indirect ones. The words arthritis, arthritic, hip dysplasia, dysplasia, joint pain, chronic pain, inflammation must not appear as things the bed acts upon. You may still speak to the OWNER of an older or stiffer dog; you may not promise the bed does anything to the condition.

### Animal products have an extra, harder rule: medicinal claims fail regardless of evidence

CAP: 'Any claim (stated or implied) that a product is effective in treating an adverse condition in animals … is likely to be seen as medicinal.' And critically: 'Medicinal claims made for an unlicensed product break the rules, regardless of any evidence you may hold.' Veterinary medicines are licensed by the Veterinary Medicines Directorate (VMD). Non-medicinal health benefits (the examples given are 'improves equine joint health' and 'repels fleas, ticks and other external parasites') are permitted only with 'robust clinical evidence' under rule 12.1.

Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/news/vetting-claims-about-products-for-animal-health.html

**Action:** This is stricter than the human-product position. For a dog bed there is no amount of evidence that would make 'treats your dog's arthritis' lawful, because the product is unlicensed. Don't let a copywriter think a study or a vet quote unlocks it — it does not.

### 'Orthopaedic' as a bare product-category descriptor is not itself a protected term, but the ASA polices what it implies

No fetched regulator source defines or restricts the word 'orthopaedic' for furniture; secondary trade commentary surfaced in search (John Ryan By Design, bedfed.org.uk) describes it as an unregulated marketing term with no industry standard — I did not fetch these, so treat that as unverified background. What IS verified: in the Origin ruling the ASA read 'Orthopaedic Back Support' as a claim of 'medically-proven orthopaedic support' and required clinical trials. So the risk sits in the surrounding words, not the noun.

Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/origin-sleep-uk-ltd-a24-1253221-origin-sleep-uk-ltd.html

**Action:** Keep 'orthopaedic' where it functions as a category/shape descriptor the market already uses and that Google Shopping shoppers search for ('Orthopaedic Memory Foam Dog Bed'), because removing it would cost you the head term. But never pair it with medical proof language — no 'medically proven orthopaedic support', 'clinically proven', 'orthopaedic-grade', 'veterinary-grade', 'vet-approved', 'designed by orthopaedic specialists' unless you hold the evidence and the actual relationship. Describe the foam by what it verifiably is instead: density, thickness, construction.

### The burden of proof sits with PawLunova and must be discharged before publication, and customer reviews do not count

Rule 3.7: marketers must 'hold documentary evidence to prove claims that consumers are likely to regard as objective and that are capable of objective substantiation.' CAP: 'self-reported consumer data or other subjective data will not be sufficient to support a claim that would otherwise require objective substantiation'; testimonials alone are 'unlikely to be considered sufficient to substantiate objective claims'; and evidence must be held BEFORE distributing or submitting ads. Whether a claim is objective is judged on how consumers are likely to interpret it, not on the advertiser's intent. Obvious exaggeration and genuine opinion ('the most comfortable bed') do not need evidence 'provided they do not materially mislead'.

Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/substantiation.html

**Action:** Every objective claim in the 64 descriptions needs a named evidence source on file before it goes live — foam density, load-bearing, waterproof rating, wash temperature, dimensions, guarantee terms. Build an evidence column into the rewrite sheet. Subjective comfort language is where the emotional register can legitimately live.

### The '100-night home trial' probably must NOT be described as a 'free trial'

CAP rule 3.26 prohibits using 'free trial' to describe 'satisfaction or your money back' offers or offers for which a non-refundable purchase is required. CAP: 'The phrase "free trial" should only be used if the product or service being offered is genuinely "free" to the consumer.' Genuine postage may be charged but not packing, handling or admin fees. The ASA upheld against HealthMail Ltd (2005) for labelling a '100% satisfied or your money back guarantee' as a free trial.

Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/promotional-marketing-subscription-traps.html

**Action:** If the customer pays £80–£200 up front and gets a refund on return, that is a money-back guarantee, not a free trial. Call it '100-night home trial — not right for your dog? Send it back for a full refund' and never 'free trial'. State who pays return carriage and whether the bed must be in returnable condition, in the copy itself.

### The 5-year no-flatten guarantee must carry its limitations in the ad, not buried in terms

CAP guidance on guarantees and warranties (rules 3.55, 3.56, 3.57 in current numbering): 'guarantee' must not be used in ways that confuse consumers about their statutory rights; ads 'must not mislead by omitting significant limitations to guarantees, particularly those affecting consumer rights'; and marketers offering money-back guarantees 'must promptly refund consumers making valid claims'. Significant limitations needing disclosure include coverage scope, conditional circumstances, criteria the consumer must fulfil, and exclusions. Full terms must be supplied before the consumer commits. Rulings cited include Alton Towers, Plumbforce Direct, Virgin Media and Orthoback/MedTech (failed to honour money-back guarantee claims).

Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/guarantees-and-warranties.html

**Action:** Define 'no-flatten' measurably (e.g. loses no more than X% of original loft under normal domestic use) or stop using it as a guarantee term — an undefined performance guarantee is a limitation problem waiting to happen. Name the exclusions in the description: chewing/destruction, commercial or kennel use, covers vs core, proof of purchase, and whether it is replacement or refund. Say plainly that it is in addition to statutory rights.

### Countdown timers, low-stock messages and 'X people viewing' are now a banned-practice risk under the DMCC Act, not just an ASA matter

The DMCC Act 2024 replaced the CPRs for practices from 6 April 2025. Banned Practice 7: 'Falsely stating that a product will only be available for a limited time, or that it will only be available on particular terms for a limited time, in order to elicit an immediate decision and deprive consumers of sufficient opportunity or time to make an informed choice.' CMA207 gives the exact failure modes: a countdown clock where 'when the time runs down, the offer does not in fact end, but continues and the countdown clock restarts'; and a pop-up saying 'Be quick! We've sold 10 in the last 5 mins' where 'while it is literally true that the stated number of people have just bought the product, the trader's stock levels are high and there is no need for consumers to hurry' — that one is caught as a misleading action through deceptive overall presentation even though the number is true. Banned Practice 5 also catches advertising at a price the trader has reasonable grounds to believe cannot be supplied in reasonable quantities for a reasonable period, without disclosing that.

Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices

**Action:** A true statement can still be unlawful if the overall presentation manufactures urgency that does not exist. Any timer must end a real offer that really stops. Any 'only 3 left' must be live inventory, and must not be shown when replacement stock is inbound. Drop 'X people are viewing this' unless it is genuinely measured AND stock is genuinely constrained. Given the brief's goal of psychological pressure, this is the highest-exposure area in the whole project.

### The CMA has already enforced on exactly these tactics and is watching the sector

CMA compliance guidance: 'Countdowns that are untrue because the sale price rolls on beyond the advertised end point are misleading'; 'Timers that start again when the webpage is refreshed or re-start immediately when the time runs out are misleading' and constitute unfair pressure. On scarcity, two distinct problems: stock claims that are simply false, and claims that have no bearing on the consumer's order because new stock is on its way. On was/now pricing: 'Such claims are misleading where they no longer reflect the item's usual price' — illustrated with a £120 price cut to £90 after two weeks and still advertised as a saving months later. The CMA investigated Wowcher over 'Running out!' and 'In high demand!' claims and secured over £4 million in refunds for Wowcher customers.

Source: https://competitionandmarkets.blog.gov.uk/2023/03/31/urgency-and-price-reduction-claims-are-your-online-tactics-legal/

**Action:** If any of the 64 products carry a permanent 'RRP £X, now £Y' strike-through where £Y is the everyday price, that is the Wowcher pattern and must be fixed before the descriptions go live — otherwise the rewrite just scales an existing exposure across the whole catalogue.

### With zero reviews of their own, the temptation to borrow competitor voice becomes a banned practice

Under the DMCC Act three categories are banned: fake reviews ('reviews that look or claim to be genuine, but are actually fake'), concealed incentivised reviews ('reviews that conceal the fact that they have been incentivised'), and false or misleading consumer review information — where 'consumer review information' means 'aggregated information in the form of overall ratings, review summaries, review counts and rankings.' Traders publishing reviews on their own websites must publish a policy prohibiting fake reviews, conduct risk assessments, run detection and investigation processes, and remove banned content; they 'remain responsible even when third parties help manage reviews.'

Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fake-reviews-cma208/short-guide-for-businesses-publishing-consumer-reviews-and-complying-with-consumer-protection-law

**Action:** The research brief for this project involves mining competitor reviews for voice and phrasing. Mining patterns of language is fine. Reproducing a competitor's customer quote on a PawLunova product page, or writing a star rating, review count or 'rated 4.8 by UK dog owners' into copy or schema when no such reviews exist, is a banned practice. Ship the 64 descriptions with no review counts, no aggregate ratings and no testimonials until real verified reviews exist. When they do, any incentivised review must be visibly disclosed.

### Testimonials carry their own evidence and permission requirements, and never substitute for substantiation

CAP rules on testimonials and endorsements: marketers must hold documentary evidence that testimonials are genuine and keep contact details for the person or organisation (rule 3.47); 'Testimonials must relate to the product advertised' and must not be taken out of context or misleadingly edited (3.48); claims within testimonials must not mislead (3.49); permission is required to feature a testimonial (3.50); ads cannot display trust marks or claim approval/endorsement from a body without authorisation (3.52); and must not falsely claim signatory status to a code of conduct (3.53). Acceptable evidence includes signed dated testimonies with contact details, email correspondence with verifiable addresses and order history, and payment system data matching the review.

Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/testimonials-and-endorsements.html

**Action:** A testimonial cannot be used to smuggle in a claim the brand could not make itself — the Bella&Toby ruling turned on precisely that. If you later quote a customer saying 'his arthritis is so much better', that claim becomes PawLunova's claim and fails.

### Origin and 'British' claims need to match where manufacture actually happens

CAP: marketers must not imply a product is 'made', 'manufactured' or 'built' in a country where this is not true, and must not use national flags or emblems to suggest UK origin if based elsewhere. Rule 3.7 requires documentary evidence for objective origin claims. Rule 3.4.2 (updated April 2025) requires ads featuring a price to include the marketer's identity, trading name if different, business address, service address if different, and business email address. Upheld cases: Wilfredandalice.co.uk (2018), UK Battery Shop (2014), 95Max.co.uk (2018), Partex Global GmbH (2022, .co.uk domain plus British flag implied UK base when German), DS Marketing Ltd (2012, Union Jack implied UK manufacture when Israeli-based). Not upheld: Vauxhall Motors (2021) — 'British Brand since 1903' was acceptable because the company was established in the UK, began manufacturing there in 1903, maintained UK operations and sold exclusively in the UK.

Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/distance-selling-location.html

**Action:** If the beds are imported and finished or shipped from the UK, you may say 'UK business', 'designed in Britain', 'dispatched from our UK warehouse' — but not 'British made' or 'made in Britain', and no Union Jack next to the product. Confirm actual manufacture location before any origin line goes into 64 descriptions at once. Also check that the PDP or footer carries the trader identity, address and email required alongside prices.

### Google Merchant Center suspends for misrepresentation without warning, which would kill the Google Shopping goal outright

Google's Misrepresentation policy prohibits 'scamming users by hiding or misrepresenting info about your business or product', including false claims and 'improbable result' pitches, 'falsely implying affiliation with, or endorsement by, another individual, organization, product, or service', and harmful health claims. Under omission of relevant information it flags 'failure to clearly and noticeably disclose the payment model and full expense' and 'failure to clearly and noticeably disclose all related conditions before and after purchase', with returns/refund policies that are unclear, missing or not easily discoverable treated as a violation. Under unavailable offers, promoted deals must still be active and the call to action must be 'easily achievable from the landing page'. Enforcement: accounts 'will be suspended upon detection and without prior warning' for egregious violations; most violations get a warning email with 7 or 28 days to fix; 'accounts are only reinstated in compelling circumstances'.

Source: https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6150127?hl=en

**Action:** The stated goal is top placement in Google Shopping. Unsubstantiated therapeutic claims are simultaneously an ASA problem and a Merchant Center health-claim/misrepresentation problem, and the Merchant Center downside is total loss of the channel. Separately, the 100-night trial and 5-year guarantee terms and the returns policy must be clearly discoverable from the product landing page — vague trial/guarantee wording is itself a disclosure violation.

### The DMCC total-price rule constrains how delivery is presented across all 64 pages

CMA207 on invitations to purchase: material information includes the total price 'including all mandatory fees, taxes, and charges', or where it cannot reasonably be calculated in advance, 'the way the total price will be calculated, to enable the consumer to calculate the price', plus any optional freight, delivery or postal charges not included. 'The practice of showing consumers an initial headline price for a product and subsequently introducing additional mandatory charges as consumers proceed with a purchase or transaction — sometimes called "drip pricing" — is prohibited.' Information must be given 'clearly, in a timely way and in a way that the consumer is likely to see it.'

Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices

**Action:** UK mainland delivery terms and any surcharge for Highlands, islands or Northern Ireland must be stated or calculable on the product page, not revealed at checkout. If copy says 'free UK delivery', it must be free to every address that line appears to cover, or the exclusion must sit next to the claim.

### The brief's explicit goal — 'emotionally and psychologically manipulated' copy — is the one instruction that cannot be followed as written

Persuasion is lawful; manufactured urgency and unevidenced outcome promises are not. CMA207's own worked example shows a literally true statement ('We've sold 10 in the last 5 mins') still being caught because the overall presentation creates pressure that the facts do not justify. The Bella&Toby ruling shows the guilt-and-rescue frame ('DON'T LET YOUR PET SUFFER') being struck down when tied to an unsubstantiated benefit. Both of those are exactly the levers this brief reaches for.

Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices

**Action:** Reframe the objective: the emotional power has to come from precision about the dog's real daily life — the struggle to get up off a hard floor, the circling before settling, the owner hearing joints click at 3am — and from removing risk (trial, guarantee, clear returns). That converts, it is defensible, and it survives an ASA complaint from a competitor. Pressure mechanics and cure promises do not.

## Hard rules

- MAY CLAIM — structural and comfort facts the product verifiably has: supportive, cushioning, weight-distributing, pressure-relieving in the descriptive sense of how foam behaves, easier to get in and out of, warm, cosy, non-slip, washable, waterproof. These are objective claims and each still needs evidence on file before publication under CAP rule 3.7. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/substantiation.html
- MAY CLAIM — 'positional, temporary relief or comfort', because CAP explicitly allows that 'a well-supported posture might provide temporary pain relief to some conditions' where evidence supports it. Do not extend it to imply the product treats a condition or that relief lasts beyond the time the dog is on the bed. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/adjustable-beds-and-chairs.html
- MAY CLAIM — subjective comfort opinion and obvious puffery ('the cosiest spot in the house'), which needs no evidence 'provided they do not materially mislead'. This is where emotional register belongs. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/substantiation.html
- MUST NOT CLAIM — that the bed treats, relieves, eases, soothes or reduces pain, whether directly or indirectly. 'Marketers of devices that are not appropriately certified as a medical device should not make claims to treat, relieve or reduce pain (directly or indirectly).' Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/health-pain.html
- MUST NOT CLAIM — that the bed acts on arthritis, arthritic pain, hip dysplasia, joint pain, chronic pain, inflammation, stiffness as a condition, or recovery from injury or surgery. Naming the condition as something the product addresses engages rule 12.2 (discouraging essential treatment). Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/health-pain.html and https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/adjustable-beds-and-chairs.html
- MUST NOT CLAIM — that the bed reduces, calms, eases or eliminates anxiety, stress, separation anxiety or nervousness. This exact claim for a dog bed was upheld against by the ASA. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/a-t-international-ltd-a22-1169887-at-international-ltd.html
- MUST NOT CLAIM — any medicinal effect on an animal, at all. For an unlicensed product 'medicinal claims … break the rules, regardless of any evidence you may hold.' No study, vet quote or trial unlocks this. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/news/vetting-claims-about-products-for-animal-health.html
- MUST NOT CLAIM — improved circulation or blood supply, reduced joint inflammation, faster recovery, or immune benefit. Named by CAP as requiring clinical substantiation that this product category does not hold. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/adjustable-beds-and-chairs.html
- 'Orthopaedic' MAY be used as a category descriptor in titles and feed data (it is the head search term), but MUST NOT be dressed in proof language: no 'medically proven', 'clinically proven', 'orthopaedic-grade', 'veterinary-grade', 'vet-approved', 'vet-recommended', 'designed by orthopaedic specialists' unless the relationship is real and the evidence is held. The ASA read 'Orthopaedic Back Support' as a claim of medically-proven support requiring clinical trials. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/origin-sleep-uk-ltd-a24-1253221-origin-sleep-uk-ltd.html
- No numeric or comparative performance claims against other beds ('3x more support', 'twice the pressure relief', 'better than ordinary beds') without held evidence — these breached CAP rule 3.33 on comparisons with identifiable competitors in the Origin ruling. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/origin-sleep-uk-ltd-a24-1253221-origin-sleep-uk-ltd.html
- Hold documentary evidence for every objective claim BEFORE the description is published, not after a complaint. Maintain an evidence column in the rewrite sheet naming the source for each factual claim. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/substantiation.html
- Never use a customer quote to make a claim the brand itself could not make. A testimonial is 'subjective' and lacks 'the robust evidence required to support objective, scientific claims'. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/a-t-international-ltd-a22-1169887-at-international-ltd.html
- Do not publish any star rating, review count, review summary or 'rated X by UK dog owners' while the store has no genuine reviews — false or misleading consumer review information is a banned practice, and 'consumer review information' expressly covers 'overall ratings, review summaries, review counts and rankings'. This includes review schema in structured data. Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fake-reviews-cma208/short-guide-for-businesses-publishing-consumer-reviews-and-complying-with-consumer-protection-law
- Do not reproduce competitor customer quotes as PawLunova testimonials. Study the language patterns; do not copy the reviews. Fake reviews are a banned practice and traders 'remain responsible even when third parties help manage reviews'. Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fake-reviews-cma208/short-guide-for-businesses-publishing-consumer-reviews-and-complying-with-consumer-protection-law
- When real testimonials exist: hold documentary evidence they are genuine, keep the person's contact details, obtain permission, keep them related to the product advertised, and do not edit them misleadingly (CAP rules 3.47–3.50). Any incentivised review must be visibly disclosed. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/testimonials-and-endorsements.html
- Do NOT call the 100-night home trial a 'free trial'. Rule 3.26 prohibits 'free trial' for 'satisfaction or your money back' offers; the term may only be used 'if the product or service being offered is genuinely free to the consumer'. Use '100-night home trial — return for a full refund if it's not right'. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/promotional-marketing-subscription-traps.html
- State the trial's conditions in the copy: who pays return carriage, what condition the bed must be in, how the refund is issued, and any excluded products. Refunds on valid claims must be paid promptly. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/guarantees-and-warranties.html
- Define 'no-flatten' in the 5-year guarantee measurably, or do not present it as a guarantee. Undefined performance guarantees create the omitted-limitation problem the ASA rules against. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/guarantees-and-warranties.html
- Disclose every significant limitation of the 5-year guarantee next to the claim, not only in terms: exclusions (chewing, destruction, commercial or kennel use), whether it covers the foam core, the cover or both, proof-of-purchase requirement, and whether remedy is repair, replacement or refund. Full terms must be available before the consumer commits. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/guarantees-and-warranties.html
- State that the guarantee is in addition to, and does not affect, the customer's statutory rights — 'guarantee' must not be used in a way that confuses consumers about their rights. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/guarantees-and-warranties.html
- No countdown timer unless a real offer genuinely ends when it expires. A timer that restarts on refresh, or where the price continues afterwards, is misleading and engages banned practice 7. Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices and https://competitionandmarkets.blog.gov.uk/2023/03/31/urgency-and-price-reduction-claims-are-your-online-tactics-legal/
- No 'only X left' unless it reflects live inventory AND stock is genuinely constrained. A true low-stock number is still misleading where replacement stock is on its way and the claim 'has no bearing on any order the consumer may place'. Source: https://competitionandmarkets.blog.gov.uk/2023/03/31/urgency-and-price-reduction-claims-are-your-online-tactics-legal/
- No 'X people are viewing this' / 'sold N in the last N minutes' social-pressure widgets. CMA207's worked example catches these even when literally true, because 'the trader's stock levels are high and there is no need for consumers to hurry'. Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices
- No 'limited time only' or 'sale ends soon' unless the stated terms genuinely end. Banned practice 7 covers 'falsely stating that a product will only be available for a limited time … in order to elicit an immediate decision'. Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices
- No permanent strike-through 'was/now' pricing where the lower figure is the usual selling price — 'such claims are misleading where they no longer reflect the item's usual price'. Audit existing RRP comparisons before scaling copy across all 64 products. Source: https://competitionandmarkets.blog.gov.uk/2023/03/31/urgency-and-price-reduction-claims-are-your-online-tactics-legal/
- Show the total price including all mandatory charges on the product page, and state any delivery surcharge for Highlands, islands or Northern Ireland alongside any 'free UK delivery' claim. Drip pricing is prohibited. Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices
- No 'British made' or 'made in Britain' and no Union Jack imagery unless the beds are actually manufactured in the UK. 'Designed in Britain', 'UK business' or 'dispatched from our UK warehouse' are available if true and evidenced. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/distance-selling-location.html
- Ensure trader identity, trading name, business address and business email are available wherever prices appear, per rule 3.4.2 as updated April 2025. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/distance-selling-location.html
- No implied endorsement by any body, brand, charity, vet practice or certification scheme that has not given it — a banned practice under the DMCC Act ('claiming that a trader … or a product has been approved, endorsed or authorised by a public or private body when … the claim is false') and separately grounds for Google Merchant Center suspension. No unauthorised trust marks or quality marks. Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices and https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6150127?hl=en

Source: https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6150127?hl=en
