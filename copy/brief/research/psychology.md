## Findings (19)

### The customer's actual sentence shape: dog first, product second, behaviour as proof

Across every competitor review page fetched, owners never lead with the product. They lead with the dog's name, age, breed and problem, then report a behavioural change as evidence. Verbatim from zooplus (2,122 reviews, 4.5/5): "Bought the large one for my 12 year old German Shepherd who had a knee operation and has arthritis. She absolutely loves it." And: "Now she jumps straight into it when we say its bedtime and she sleeps the whole night through." From George Barclay Trustpilot, Susan Brady (5 stars): "This time I have purchased the memory foam mattress as he is arthritic and sometimes finds it a little difficult to get out of the walled ones." From Lee (5 stars): "Our dog took to it straight away and loves resting his head on the raised sides." From Donna Pitchford (5 stars): "Our puppy got straight in the bed. He loves it." The recurring verbs are: took to it, jumps straight in, sleeps the whole night through, absolutely loves, settled. The recurring nouns are: stiff hips, back legs, joints, bedtime, mornings. Owners also give physical specifics unprompted — "My black labrador is male, 44kg. He does not fit well on the largest size."

Source: zooplus 2,122-review page (fetched); uk.trustpilot.com/review/www.georgebarclay.co.uk (fetched)

**Action:** Write every description's opening paragraph in this order: (1) the dog and the moment of difficulty, (2) what the bed does at that moment, (3) the observable change. Do NOT open with foam density or dimensions. Worked example: "If your dog circles three or four times before dropping onto his bed with a grunt, he is looking for a soft spot that is not there. The Ashbourne's 10cm memory foam core holds his hips level while he sleeps, so the shoulder and elbow he is lying on are not taking his full weight all night." Build a mandatory verb list for copywriters drawn from the reviews — took to it, settled, jumps straight in, sleeps the night through, gets up easier — and ban marketing verbs like "revolutionise", "transform", "pamper".

### Loss aversion: sell the cost of the floor he is on now, not the benefit of the bed

Kahneman and Tversky's loss aversion predicts a described loss outweighs an equivalent described gain. The reviews show owners already hold the loss framing themselves — they describe the status quo as the painful thing. zooplus: "We are hoping that this bed will help her arthritic hips, as she can no longer jump up to her spot on the sofa." George Barclay: "he is arthritic and sometimes finds it a little difficult to get out of the walled ones." Note what is NOT available: the UK VetCompass study of 455,557 dogs found an annual period prevalence of appendicular osteoarthritis of just 2.5% (~200,000 UK dogs), with age over eight a risk factor and the condition occupying 11.4% of an affected dog's lifespan. The widely circulated "80% of senior dogs have arthritis" figure is not supported by this UK primary-care data.

Source: Anderson et al., Scientific Reports 2018 (VetCompass); zooplus and George Barclay review pages (fetched)

**Action:** Frame the alternative, not the product. The unit of loss is a night, and there are 365 of them a year. Worked example: "A dog sleeps between twelve and fourteen hours a day. On a flat cushion that has gone hard in the middle, that is twelve hours a day of his hip bearing his own weight against the floor. Over a year that is roughly 4,500 hours." Use the arithmetic, not an adjective. NEVER cite the 80% arthritis statistic; if a prevalence number is wanted, cite VetCompass properly or use none at all.

### Anticipated regret and guilt are the live emotion in this category — and there is a hard ethical ceiling on using it

Peer-reviewed work on dog owners found guilt and work-family conflict at levels similar to those in human family studies, with guilt tracking the discrepancy between an owner's actual and ideal self as a dog owner; owners reported worry and guilt about whether they were sufficiently meeting their dog's needs. Separately, an Attest survey found 24% of new pet owners have some regret, 15.6% wishing they had thought it through more. The UK Frontiers study shows the bond owners describe: "How I truly consider him to be one of my best friends", "I never imagined that I could love the way I love [my dog]", "The unbreakable bond between me and my dog". This is a genuine and already-sore nerve. Copy that presses it with an accusation ("a good owner would have done this already") is exploiting a documented anxiety disorder correlate, not persuading.

Source: Human-Animal Interactions, 'Dog owners: Disenfranchised guilt'; Attest pet owner regret survey; Frontiers in Vet Science 2024 (fetched)

**Action:** Use forward-looking relief, never backward-looking blame. The sentence shape is a future memory, not a reproach. Worked example (use): "You will not get these years back, and neither will he. A hundred nights from now you will either know this bed was right for him, or you will have sent it back and be no worse off." Worked example (ban): "Still letting your arthritic dog sleep on the floor?" or "What kind of owner leaves their dog in pain?" Rule for copywriters: never imply the reader has already failed their dog.

### Anthropomorphism: owners buy for the dog the way they buy for themselves, so borrow the human bedding vocabulary

A two-stage study of 75 in-depth interviews plus 745 dog owners found how people shop for their dogs is systematically related to the nature of their relationship with the dog, and that dog owners buy for their dogs in ways similar to how they buy for themselves. The UK Frontiers study found owners routinely describe dogs as family members and compare them to children. This is why 'memory foam', 'mattress', 'orthopaedic' and 'topper' work as category words — they import an existing, trusted human product schema the buyer already knows how to evaluate.

Source: Journal of Targeting/Springer, 'Dimensions of the dog-human relationship'; Frontiers in Vet Science 2024 (fetched)

**Action:** Deliberately use the human-mattress lexicon: mattress, topper, support layer, firmness, pressure points, sagging, the bit in the middle that goes hard. Give the dog interiority in the sentence without putting words in his mouth. Worked example: "You would not sleep on a mattress that had gone flat in the middle for five years. He is doing it because he has no choice about where he sleeps — you make that choice for him." Avoid cartoon anthropomorphism (dogs 'dreaming of' the bed, first-person dog voice); the Frontiers quotes show owners are sincere, not whimsical, about the bond.

### Endowment effect + the 100-night trial: describe the dog already owning it

The endowment effect means people value what they possess above an identical thing they do not. A trial creates possession before payment is final. Category benchmark: 94.1% of mattresses carry at least a 100-night trial, so the trial is table stakes — the differentiation is in how it is described, not that it exists. Online mattress return rates run around 15-20% industry-wide with well-run brands at 5-8%, so honest trial language is commercially survivable.

Source: Sunday Rest mattress return rate data; retail trial-period benchmarks (search); endowment effect literature

**Action:** Do not write '100-night trial' as a bullet. Narrate possession and the act of return. Worked example: "Sleep him on it for a hundred nights. Wash the cover, let him dig at it, let him do the thing where he drags it across the kitchen. If by night one hundred he is still choosing the sofa, email us and we will collect it — you do not need to have kept the box." The clause 'you do not need to have kept the box' is doing more work than the number 100, because it removes the unstated objection Baymard-style research says users silently carry. Only write it if it is true.

### Specificity is the single cheapest credibility device, and this category's buyers reward numbers

NN/g's Trust and Credibility report (53 recommendations, 350+ sites tested incl. UK) identifies providing appropriate levels of product detail, proactively addressing common product questions, and justifying costs as core trust mechanisms. Baymard's PDP research dedicates 8 guidelines across 70 pages to product descriptions specifically, noting "it's often on the product page where users make up their mind". The review corpus confirms buyers think in numbers unprompted: "44kg", "12 year old German Shepherd", "3 years", "6 weeks on", "less than 6 months of use".

Source: https://www.nngroup.com/reports/ecommerce-ux-trust-and-credibility/ (fetched); https://baymard.com/research/product-page (fetched)

**Action:** Replace every evaluative adjective with a measurement or a named mechanism. Worked replacements: "premium supportive foam" → "10cm of 55kg/m³ high-density memory foam over a 5cm reflex base"; "easy to clean" → "the cover unzips on three sides and washes at 40°C; the inner liner is waterproof so the foam never gets wet"; "durable" → "we guarantee the foam will not lose more than 10% of its height for five years". Rule: if a sentence contains an adjective and no number, a copywriter must either add the number or delete the sentence.

### Concreteness: name the moment, the sound and the surface — not the feeling

The reviews that carry conviction are the ones anchored to a specific observable scene, not a feeling: "Now she jumps straight into it when we say its bedtime", "This bed is the perfect height for her to get on and off", "she can no longer jump up to her spot on the sofa", "loves resting his head on the raised sides". Abstract praise ("absolutely loves it") appears constantly but is never the persuasive part — it is the specific staging around it that is.

Source: zooplus review corpus and George Barclay Trustpilot (both fetched)

**Action:** Every description gets at least three concrete sensory anchors: a time of day, a physical action the dog performs, and a tactile property. Worked example: "The bolster is 18cm high and filled firm, not fibre-loose — so when he props his chin on it at ten o'clock it stays where he put it, instead of flattening out by midnight and leaving his head on the floor." Ban abstract nouns as sentence subjects: comfort, luxury, wellbeing, quality. Use surfaces, heights, temperatures, times.

### Price reframing per night is empirically strong, but only if the daily figure lands in trivial territory

Gourville's 'Pennies-a-Day' (JCR 1998, 24(4):395-408) found temporal reframing shifts the comparison set: framed as "an ongoing contribution of 85 cents per day", 52% of participants agreed, versus 30% for "a total contribution of $300 per year" — the same money. Per-issue magazine framing outperformed annual framing by 10-40% on response. The mechanism is that the small daily figure retrieves small daily expenses (a coffee) as the comparison standard, while the aggregate retrieves large infrequent expenses (a bill).

Source: Gourville 1998, Journal of Consumer Research; https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-science-behind-behavior/201904/the-powerful-influence-pennies-day-price-offers (fetched)

**Action:** Anchor against the 5-year foam guarantee, not the trial, because that gives the trivially small number. A £149 bed over 1,825 guaranteed nights is 8p a night; a £249 bed is 14p a night. Worked example: "£149 works out at eight pence a night across the five years we guarantee the foam for — less than the dog food in a single bowl." Compute this per product and put it as a single line near the price, not in the hero. Do NOT do this on the £49-£79 lines where the per-night figure is already negligible and the maths reads as padding.

### The pratfall effect is available to PawLunova, but only after competence is established

Aronson's 1966 experiment: a contestant who answered 90% of questions correctly and then spilled coffee was rated significantly more appealing than the same flawless performer. The documented boundary condition is decisive — "Spilling coffee after winning builds likeability; spilling after failing just looks clumsy." The effect backfires when the flaw is fake (humblebrags), when the confession precedes proof of competence, when the weakness is fatal to the product, or when the flaw is borrowed from a competitor. Ogilvy's version: "Tell your prospective client your weakness before they notice them. This will make you more credible when you boast about your strong points." Critically, the 1-star reviews fetched tell you which real flaws buyers will discover anyway: covers ripping ("6 weeks on, they're ruined! The covers have ripped on both beds"), holes within six months, wrong-size covers, zips failing in the wash ("the zips didn't hold up at all in the wash"), foam shedding chunks, and sizing disappointment ("My black labrador is male, 44kg. He does not fit well on the largest size").

Source: https://www.stfo.io/articles/pratfall-effect-marketing/ (fetched); Ogilvy credibility principle; 1-star reviews on George Barclay Trustpilot and zooplus (fetched)

**Action:** Place the admission AFTER the specification block, never before it, and make it a real trade-off with a reason. Worked example for a memory foam line: "It is heavy. The large is 7.2kg, which is not a bed you will casually carry upstairs — that weight is the foam density doing its job, and a lighter bed would be a thinner one." Worked example for sizing: "Our large fits a 30kg labrador who sleeps curled. If yours sleeps flat out on his side, order the XL — more returns come back to us for this one reason than any other." Each product needs exactly one honest flaw, and it must be the flaw a buyer would otherwise find in a review.

### Social proof with zero reviews: substitute specificity and mechanism, and never fabricate

Social proof is Cialdini's most-studied ecommerce lever and has the largest effect precisely when uncertainty is high — which is exactly PawLunova's £80-£200 considered purchase. CXL's applied guidance for a store with none: "If you have no social proof yet, give your product away to a few credible users first, or borrow authority through a relevant quote, rather than fake it." Fabricating is also now a strict-liability offence in the UK (see rules). NN/g's trust report lists company information, demonstrated expertise, transparent values, proactive answering of product questions, pricing justification and well-defined support policies as trust mechanisms that operate independently of reviews.

Source: https://cxl.com/blog/cialdinis-principles-persuasion/ (fetched); https://www.nngroup.com/reports/ecommerce-ux-trust-and-credibility/ (fetched)

**Action:** Run four substitutes while review volume is zero. (1) Founder-origin specificity: "We started making these after our own collie, Maggie, stopped being able to get up off a flat cushion at eleven." (2) Mechanism transparency: publish the actual foam density and the supplier spec. (3) Policy as proof: "We have refunded every trial return since we opened, no questions asked — ask us and we will tell you how many." (4) A seeding programme: send 20 beds to UK owners of senior dogs on condition of an honest review, disclosed as gifted. Ban 'X people are viewing this', 'Over 10,000 happy dogs', 'Bestseller' badges and star ratings until real, verifiable data exists.

### Cialdini's levers ranked for this specific purchase — authority and unity beat scarcity

CXL's applied breakdown of the 7 principles gives mechanisms with mostly qualitative results: social proof (ModCloth 'Be the Buyer' badge "doubled the rate of conversion rate compared to items with no badge"), liking (PetRelocation's bio-heavy About Us page highlighting staff love of dogs "increased the company's likeability, which in turn boosted the conversion rate"), unity (asking for 'advice' rather than 'opinions' significantly increased intent), authority, reciprocity, commitment, scarcity. For PawLunova, scarcity is the lever to switch OFF: it is the one the DMCC Act directly polices and the one a store with no review history cannot substantiate.

Source: https://cxl.com/blog/cialdinis-principles-persuasion/ (fetched)

**Action:** Prioritise in this order: (1) Unity — shared-identity language for UK owners of ageing dogs; (2) Authority — real credentials only (name the foam spec, the test standard, any vet who actually reviewed the range, the UK manufacturing location if true); (3) Liking — a genuine About page naming the actual dogs in the business, which is the PetRelocation mechanism; (4) Commitment — the trial IS the micro-commitment, so make the first step small and reversible; (5) Reciprocity — a genuinely useful free 'Is your dog's bed the problem?' guide, not a discount code. Do not deploy scarcity at all.

### Schwartz's awareness stages map directly onto PawLunova's 64-product / Google Shopping split

Schwartz's five states — Unaware, Problem Aware, Solution Aware, Product Aware, Most Aware — require a different opening line each, because "a message written for one stage reads as noise to a prospect in any other", and the copywriter "cannot create desire, only channel existing desire". Google Shopping traffic arrives Solution Aware or Product Aware (they typed 'orthopaedic dog bed large'); organic blog and category traffic arrives Problem Aware ('why does my old dog struggle to get up').

Source: Schwartz, Breakthrough Advertising via breakthroughadvertisingbook.com; https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6324468 (fetched)

**Action:** Give every product page a two-tier opening. Tier 1, the first 160 characters that Google truncates to and that a Solution Aware shopper reads, must be plain and specific: "Orthopaedic memory foam dog bed with a 10cm high-density core and a washable waterproof cover. 100-night trial, 5-year no-flatten guarantee." Tier 2, below the fold, does the Problem Aware emotional work for organic arrivals. Never lead a Shopping-destined field with the emotional hook — it reads as noise to someone who already knows what they want and it violates Google's description rules.

### Google Merchant Center imposes hard limits that override copywriting instincts

Title attribute: 1-150 characters, most important information in the first 70. Explicitly prohibited: "price, sale price, sale dates, shipping, delivery date, other time-related information, or your company's name", capital letters for emphasis, foreign characters for stylistic purposes, extra whitespace, HTML tags and gimmicky symbols. Description attribute: 1-5,000 characters, "List the most important details in the first 160 - 500 characters". Explicitly prohibited: promotional text of the same kinds, links to stores or websites, product comparisons such as "even better than X", all-caps for emphasis ("Capitalized text is common in spam and untrustworthy product listing"), category-hierarchy references, and descriptions of compatible products, accessories or business history.

Source: https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6324415 (fetched); https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6324468 (fetched)

**Action:** Maintain two separate copy assets per product. The feed title formula: [Brand] [Product name] [Bed type] [Material] [Size] [Colour] — e.g. "PawLunova Ashbourne Orthopaedic Dog Bed, Memory Foam, Large 100x70cm, Slate Grey", keeping the discriminating terms inside 70 characters. The feed description: first 500 characters are material, dimensions, foam density, cover spec and washability only. The 100-night trial and 5-year guarantee are time-related and promotional — they belong on the PDP and in ad copy, NOT in the feed title or the feed description. No emoji, no ALL CAPS, no "Best", no "£" figures, no "free delivery" in either field.

### UK buyers of dog products are primed on cost shock — transparency about price is itself persuasive

The Frontiers 2024 UK study found 52% of owners said vet costs exceeded expectations and 33% said buying/rehoming costs were higher than expected, with surprise about vet costs INCREASING with dog age. Verbatim owner comments: "Vets have gone very greedy" and surprise at "how everything seems to be a £100 minimum." NN/g's trust report lists clearly displaying prices and justifying costs as distinct trust mechanisms.

Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/veterinary-science/articles/10.3389/fvets.2024.1331793/full (fetched); https://www.nngroup.com/reports/ecommerce-ux-trust-and-credibility/ (fetched)

**Action:** Pre-empt the £100-minimum flinch by justifying the cost against a named alternative the buyer already resents. Worked example: "£149 is not a cheap dog bed. It is also roughly one consultation and a course of anti-inflammatories. We are not suggesting it replaces either — but a bed that holds his joints off the floor for five years is a one-off cost, and the vet is not." This must stop short of a medical claim (see rules). Also state delivery cost inline, never at checkout.

### Identity framing: 'the kind of owner who' works here because UK owners already self-describe in these terms

Cialdini's unity principle turns on shared identity, and CXL's applied version is in-group language and co-creation. The Frontiers UK corpus shows the identity is already articulated by owners themselves: "How I truly consider him to be one of my best friends", "The unbreakable bond between me and my dog", with owners describing their lives as organised around their dogs' needs. Note that the strongest version is descriptive (recognition) rather than prescriptive (a test the reader might fail).

Source: Frontiers in Vet Science 2024 (fetched); https://cxl.com/blog/cialdinis-principles-persuasion/ (fetched)

**Action:** Use recognition, not qualification. Worked example (use): "You are the sort of person who already knows which of his back legs he favours getting up. This is the bed for that." Worked example (ban): "Only serious dog owners invest in proper orthopaedic support." The first flatters observation the reader genuinely has; the second manufactures a status threat. Also mine the specific UK-owner in-group markers from the review corpus — the dog dragging the bed round the kitchen, the sofa spot he can no longer reach, the 40°C wash.

### Risk reversal must name the friction, not just the policy

NN/g identifies well-defined support processes and policies and proactively addressing common product questions as trust mechanisms. The 1-star reviews fetched show what buyers are actually afraid of, and none of it is the refund amount: wrong size arriving ("opened the covers to put on and they were the wrong size", "After recieving the wrong item expected entirely"), and durability failing just outside a return window ("6 weeks on, they're ruined", "less than 6 months of use"). A guarantee that answers the price but not the hassle leaves the real objection standing.

Source: https://www.nngroup.com/reports/ecommerce-ux-trust-and-credibility/ (fetched); 1-star reviews, uk.trustpilot.com/review/www.georgebarclay.co.uk (fetched)

**Action:** Write the guarantee as a sequence of removed frictions, each in its own short sentence. Worked example: "If the size is wrong, we send the right one and collect the other — you do not pay the difference in postage either way. If the foam loses its shape inside five years, we replace the insert, not just the cover. You do not need the original packaging and you do not need to have kept the receipt; the order is in our system." Every clause must be independently true and operationally deliverable before it is published.

### ASA/CAP sets a precise, usable line for what a dog bed may claim about pain

This is the most directly actionable compliance finding. CAP advice on beds and chairs states "a well-supported posture might provide temporary pain relief to some conditions" and that it may be acceptable to claim products offer "positional, temporary relief or comfort to sufferers of conditions, providing that robust evidence is held" — but marketers "should not imply that the product treats a condition, or that the relief or comfort is any more than temporary." Rulings upheld against bed and chair advertisers include Niagara Therapy (July 2005, "ideal for sufferers from Arthritis, High Blood Pressure and Back Pain"), Willowbrook (Aug 2009, reclining chairs recommended for arthritis/back pain went beyond temporary positional relief) and Dreamwell (April 2012). For animals specifically, CAP states "Any claim (stated or implied) that a product is effective in treating an adverse condition in animals is likely to be seen as medicinal" and "Medicinal claims made for an unlicensed product break the rules, regardless of any evidence you may hold." Rulings exist against Aviform (equine joint health) and Calinnova.

Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/adjustable-beds-and-chairs.html (fetched); https://www.asa.org.uk/news/vetting-claims-about-products-for-animal-health.html (fetched)

**Action:** Adopt a fixed safe/unsafe phrase list. SAFE, if evidence is held: "supports his joints in a level position while he sleeps", "distributes weight so pressure is not concentrated on one hip", "many owners of older dogs tell us their dog settles faster on a firmer, level surface". UNSAFE, remove on sight: "relieves arthritis", "eases joint pain", "therapeutic", "clinically proven", "vet recommended" (unless a named vet genuinely recommends it and you hold the evidence), "reduces inflammation", "helps him heal", "treats hip dysplasia", "pain relief". Keep any comfort claim explicitly positional and explicitly temporary. Every product page needs this run over it before publication.

### The DMCC Act 2024 makes several classic conversion tactics strict-liability offences with turnover-scale penalties

Banned practices now include: fake reviews and concealing that a review was incentivised; countdown timers unless they "genuinely reflect the situation" (it may be misleading if the timer reaches zero and the price does not increase, or a near-identical promotion starts shortly after); false scarcity such as unsubstantiated stock indicators or "Running out!"; false urgency; fake popularity claims such as "In high demand" without factual verification; drip pricing (mandatory fees not in the headline price); misleading was/now reference pricing where the item was not actually sold at the stated price; pre-ticked boxes; and making opt-out "unnecessarily complicated or lengthy". Fake review provisions sit on the Schedule 20 blacklist, meaning the CMA does not have to prove the practice affected the average consumer's transactional decision. Penalties reach up to 10% of global turnover plus mandatory consumer redress.

Source: https://www.ashurstperkinscoie.com/en/insights/mind-the-nudge-the-dmccs-crackdown-on-manipulative-online-design/ (fetched); CMA208 Fake reviews guidance

**Action:** Hard-block these in the build, not just in the copy brief: no countdown timers of any kind, no "only 3 left", no "X people are viewing", no seeded or incentivised reviews without prominent disclosure, no "RRP £299" unless the product was genuinely sold at £299, no delivery surcharge revealed at checkout. If urgency is wanted, use only genuine, substantiated facts — a real dated price change, a real production run ending — and keep the written evidence on file.

### Trust signals have a ceiling — more badges convert worse

Search-surfaced analysis attributed to Baymard across 147 ecommerce sites reports trust signals lifting conversions 15-30% on average, but pages carrying seven or more trust-signal TYPES converting 8% worse than pages with one to three, because excess signals create visual noise. This is consistent with NN/g's finding that information overload obscures material information and with the DMCC's treatment of information overload as a choice-information violation. Caveat: I could not verify the 147-site figure on Baymard's own page, which paywalls the detail — treat the direction as sound and the precise number as unconfirmed.

## Hard rules

- Never claim the bed relieves, eases, treats or cures arthritis, joint pain, hip dysplasia or any condition. CAP permits only 'positional, temporary relief or comfort' from a well-supported posture, and only if robust evidence is held; anything implying treatment of a condition in an animal is a medicinal claim and breaks the rules regardless of evidence held (ASA/CAP, adjustable beds and chairs; ASA/CAP, animal health products).
- Banned words and phrases across all 64 descriptions: relieves pain, eases arthritis, therapeutic, clinically proven, reduces inflammation, helps healing, vet approved / vet recommended (unless a named vet genuinely recommends it and the evidence is on file), medical-grade, orthopaedic treatment. 'Orthopaedic' as a category noun describing the bed type is acceptable; 'orthopaedic therapy' or 'orthopaedic treatment' is not.
- Do not cite the '80% of senior dogs have arthritis' figure. UK VetCompass data on 455,557 dogs found a 2.5% annual period prevalence of appendicular osteoarthritis. Either cite that study properly or use no prevalence statistic at all.
- No countdown timers, no 'only X left', no 'X people are viewing this', no 'selling fast', no 'in high demand' and no invented urgency of any kind. These are named choice-pressure violations under the DMCC Act 2024, with penalties up to 10% of global turnover.
- No fabricated reviews, no star ratings, no review counts and no 'over N happy customers' until real verifiable data exists. Fake reviews sit on the Schedule 20 blacklist, so the CMA does not need to prove consumer harm. Incentivised or gifted reviews must be prominently disclosed as such.
- No 'RRP' or 'was/now' price unless the product was genuinely sold at that price for a meaningful period. No mandatory fee — delivery, surcharge, handling — revealed later than the headline price.
- Google Shopping feed title: maximum 150 characters, discriminating terms inside the first 70, formula [Brand] [Product name] [Bed type] [Material] [Size] [Colour]. No price, no sale dates, no delivery or shipping text, no time-related claims, no ALL CAPS for emphasis, no emoji or decorative symbols, no HTML.
- Google Shopping feed description: first 160-500 characters carry material, dimensions, foam density, cover spec and washability only. No links, no company history, no 'better than X' comparisons, no promotional or time-related text (this excludes the 100-night trial and the 5-year guarantee from the feed description — they live on the PDP only), no ALL CAPS, no category-hierarchy strings.
- Every sentence containing an evaluative adjective must also contain a number, a measurement or a named mechanism, or be deleted. No abstract noun may be the subject of a sentence: comfort, luxury, wellbeing, quality, pampering.
- Open every description with the dog and the moment of difficulty, then the mechanism, then the observable change. Never open with foam specification or dimensions. Match the customer's own verbs, taken from the competitor review corpus: took to it, settled, jumps straight in, sleeps the whole night through, gets up easier.
- Each product carries exactly one honest admitted flaw, placed after the specification block, never before it, and it must be a real trade-off with a stated reason — weight, firmness, footprint, sizing. No fake flaws or humblebrags.
- Guilt may be used only as forward-looking relief, never as backward-looking blame. Never imply the reader has already failed their dog, and never use accusatory question headlines ('Still letting your dog sleep on the floor?').
- Identity copy must be recognition, not qualification. Describe what the reader already notices about their dog; never construct a status test they could fail ('only serious owners...').
- Per-night price reframing is anchored to the 5-year foam guarantee (1,825 nights) and stated as a single line near the price. Apply it only to products at £99 and above; omit it on the £49-£79 lines.
- Maximum three trust elements per product page — the 100-night trial, the 5-year no-flatten guarantee, UK delivery. Do not stack payment badges, seals, award graphics or 'as seen in' strips on top; excess trust signals reduce conversion.
- Every guarantee and returns clause must be independently true and operationally deliverable before it is published. If PawLunova will not actually collect a returned bed without its original packaging, the copy does not say so.
- UK English throughout: orthopaedic, colour, 40°C, centimetres, kilograms, £. Use UK-mainland delivery language and never imply coverage the store does not offer.
